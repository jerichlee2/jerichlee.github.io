Adjoint ZBLL: Reading the Last Layer Early

If you can plan a cross and sometimes track your first F2L pair, you already know the starting idea: predict what your moves will do before you make them. My Adjoint ZB Compiler (AZBC) notes ask how much further that prediction could go— all the way to choosing the last-layer algorithm during inspection.

You only need familiar F2L and OLL/PLL ideas to follow this explanation, not full ZBLL or advanced math. The full inspection method is a research proposal, not a demonstrated way to plan every solve in 15 seconds.

1. What are we trying to plan?

The proposed solve has six chunks. A chunk is a complete move sequence, not a single turn:

  1. 1 Cross
  2. 2 First pair
  3. 3 Second pair
  4. 4 Third pair
  5. 5 ZBLS
  6. 6 ZBLL

ZBLS solves the last F2L pair while orienting the last-layer edges. That sets up ZBLL, which finishes the last layer in one algorithm. Any needed AUF (a U-layer adjustment before or after an algorithm) belongs in the plan too.

Normally, you reach a case, recognize it, then choose an algorithm. Here, the goal is to recognize the case you will reach while the cube is still scrambled. “Compiler” means turning those predictions into one exact sequence of familiar algorithms. It is not a new algorithm set replacing ZBLL.

2. Follow F2L forward

Start with a fixed cross color and front face. Choose your cross, then work out where each unsolved pair will land. Choose the first insertion, update the remaining pairs, and repeat until only the last pair remains.

The notes use sticker addresses: one chosen sticker on a known corner and one on its matching edge. Following a sticker tells you both where its piece goes and how it is oriented. Speffz letters are just a shorthand for those addresses; you can start with plain names such as “the U-facing sticker at UR.”

The exact moves matter. Two insertions can solve the same pair but move the other pieces differently. Include setups and rotations, and stick to that choice when making later predictions.

3. Send the question backward

Tracking every last-layer piece through every F2L move would add a lot of work. Instead, choose a future position you need to recognize and ask: “Which sticker on the original scramble will end up there?”

That change in viewpoint is what “adjoint” means here. Forward tracking follows a piece toward its destination. A backward query starts at the destination and finds the piece that will arrive.

To answer “what will be at UF?”, mentally trace that position through U′ and inspect the current UR sticker. For a longer sequence, undo the moves in reverse order on the position you are tracing.

How that scales to cross + three pairs

Trace the future position backward through the third pair’s inverse, then the second pair’s inverse, then the first pair’s inverse, then the cross’s inverse. To predict a position after ZBLS, first trace through the chosen ZBLS algorithm’s inverse, then through that same F2L prefix. Changing any planned moves means recalculating the affected queries.

4. Ask only what the next step needs

Before ZBLS: which edges are flipped?

After cross + three pairs, five edges lie outside the solved cross and pairs: the last F2L edge and the four last-layer edges. Your tracking already tells you the last-pair edge’s position and orientation. What you still need is the orientation pattern of the other four—not their identities yet.

The notes show that three orientation checks suffice: the cube’s even-flip constraint determines the fourth. Trace those checks backward to the scramble, read them there, and use the predicted pair state and edge orientations to choose an exact ZBLS algorithm.

This depends on using a consistent edge-orientation convention, including when an edge is in the open F2L slot. When learning, check all four and use the extra observation to catch mistakes.

After ZBLS: which ZBLL case will appear?

Now F2L will be solved and the last-layer edges will be oriented. The next query is three complete corner observations plus two edge identities. Pull those observations backward through your exact ZBLS and F2L moves.

For each corner, trace one chosen sticker-position address backward. Then inspect the whole corner at that address, noting which color is on the traced facelet. That gives you both its identity and orientation.

  • Three corners reveal their identities and twists. The missing corner is the remaining identity, and its twist is constrained by the other three.
  • Two edges leave only two possible arrangements of the remaining edges. Only one matches the corner permutation’s parity, so the legal cube state picks the last two for you.

That determines the last layer, including the information needed for recognition and AUF. “Five probes” does not mean five colors: each corner observation reads a whole corner’s identity and orientation. Nor does mathematical sufficiency mean it is easy to decode quickly.

The idea to take away

Follow the pieces you need for F2L forward. When a later recognition question becomes relevant, trace that question backward to the scramble. The math explains which information is enough; the open question is how much of that process a human can learn to do accurately within inspection.
