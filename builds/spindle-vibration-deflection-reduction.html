Spindle Vibration & Deflection Reduction

Excessive vibration in high-speed milling spindles degrades cut quality, accelerates tool wear, and shortens machine life. In this industry-sponsored capstone project, our team investigated vibration in the vertical spindle of Peddinghaus Corporation’s PeddiSubX-1120 steel mill.

Operational testing and finite-element modeling pointed to bending at the motor-end overhang. We proposed a floating-bearing-stack modification that raised the first simulated modal frequency from 307.17 Hz to 383.27 Hz, approximately 24.8%. The result is a design prediction, not a measured post-installation reduction in vibration.

I later revisited the result with a stepped-beam hand model to understand how geometry, restraint, and distributed mass produce that bending mode. The original project work and the retrospective calculations are presented separately below. Rev B adds a rotordynamic sensitivity check, retaining the original bending calculations and separately examining rotary inertia and gyroscopic coupling.

Process

The project began with on-site vibration testing at Peddinghaus’ manufacturing facility. A tri-axial accelerometer recorded housing motion during free-spinning tests from 250 to 3000 rpm and during selected short- and long-tool cutting tests.

Wavelet analysis showed a persistent band near 310 Hz in unloaded operation. Long-tool cutting produced multiple, speed-dependent frequency bands, and portions of those recordings saturated. Impact testing was inconclusive, so the report does not establish a measured full mode shape.

We developed an approximate spindle model in ANSYS Mechanical and refined its contacts and constraints. Its first frequency, 307.17 Hz, was about 0.9% below the unloaded band. The simulated shape concentrated bending at the motor-end tail, guiding comparisons of bracing, material changes, and bearing arrangements.

That agreement is frequency correlation after model refinement, not independent mode-shape validation. The loaded simulation was not successfully validated against the cutting tests.

Original FEA & design recommendation

The preferred concept modified the floating bearing stack and moved it 22 mm toward the drive. This changed the spindle’s support conditions while pursuing the sponsor’s goals of a practical, low-cost solution with limited maintenance.

The report labels the best-performing case “Modified 2x.” Its text is inconsistent about whether this means two bearings total or two additional bearings. I retain the case label rather than infer an exact stack configuration without the original CAD and joint definitions.

For a six-flute tool at 3000 rpm, shaft rotation is 50 Hz and the fundamental tooth-passing frequency is 300 Hz. Moving a mode above that fundamental is useful separation, but higher harmonics and cutting instability remain possible. A frequency shift alone does not establish lower vibration amplitude or chatter-free operation.

Simplified hand calculations: the motor-end overhang

These September 2026 retrospective calculations explain the bending mechanism observed in the spring 2025 project’s simulation. They were not used to choose the original design. Starting with a uniform cantilever gives a first estimate of 255.2 Hz; retaining the stepped geometry and distributed inertia brings the estimate to roughly 334–337 Hz. This nonrotating baseline is unchanged in Rev B.

The local model begins at the floating bearing and retains a 410 mm overhang, including the gear and tail. I use an ideal lateral clamp, \(w(0)=w'(0)=0\), to represent a rigid limiting support. This constrains bending displacement and slope, not spin about the shaft axis. “Floating” refers to axial freedom; it does not mean that radial support is absent.

From bending flexibility to effective stiffness

Apply a conceptual transverse force \(P\) at the tail. With \(M(s)=P(L-s)\), the strain energy gives the tail compliance. For section endpoints \(a_j,b_j\), measured in meters:

\[ I_j=\frac{\pi D_j^4}{64},\qquad C_{LL}=\frac{\delta_L}{P}=\sum_j\frac{(L-a_j)^3-(L-b_j)^3}{3E_jI_j},\qquad k_L=C_{LL}^{-1}. \]

The five sections give \(C_{LL}=3.24399\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m/N}\) and \(k_L=3.08262\times10^6\ \mathrm{N/m}\). An illustrative 100 N tail force therefore produces 32.44 µm of local tail displacement. This is neither a measured milling load nor a prediction of cutting-tool deflection.

The aluminum stem contributes about 71.5% of this model’s bending compliance; the steel root neck contributes 23.3%, and the large gear just 0.34%. The most visually prominent component is not necessarily the span that controls flexibility.

For a uniform solid beam, \(f_n\propto D L^{-2}\sqrt{E/\rho}\). That first-pass scaling helps explain why overhang geometry and restraint deserve attention before swapping between steel grades with similar elastic properties.

Rayleigh estimate: use the mass that moves

Normalize the static tip-load shape as \(\phi(s)=v(s)/v(L)\). Its squared displacement weights each section’s contribution to kinetic energy:

\[ m_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_j\int_{a_j}^{b_j}\rho_j A_j\phi(s)^2\,ds+m_r\phi(s_r)^2, \qquad f_R=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k_L}{m_{\mathrm{eff}}}}. \]

The modeled overhang weighs 10.57 kg, but its tip-normalized generalized mass is only 0.688433 kg. Combined with the effective stiffness, that gives \(f_R=336.8\ \mathrm{Hz}\).

The 6.44 kg gear sits near a nearly stationary root and contributes about 0.0098 kg to this modal mass. The 0.60 kg terminal step moves much more and contributes about 0.44 kg. Putting the entire assembly mass on a spring at the tip would describe a different motion.

“Effective mass” here is the generalized mass for a mode normalized to unit tail motion, not the ANSYS base-excitation participation metric.

Two stations, two local modes

A two-coordinate Rayleigh–Ritz reduction retains gear-center displacement at 95 mm and tail displacement at 410 mm. Unit-load beam flexibility supplies the interpolation, rather than assuming two independent lumped masses:

\[ H(s,\xi)=\int_0^{\min(s,\xi)}\frac{(s-u)(\xi-u)}{E(u)I(u)}\,du,\qquad C_{ij}=H(s_i,s_j),\qquad \mathbf K=\mathbf C^{-1}. \]

Integrating the distributed mass with the same interpolation gives a coupled mass matrix. The resulting matrices and free-vibration equation are:

\[ \mathbf K=10^6\begin{bmatrix}593.53793 & -22.53896\\-22.53896 & 3.938513\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{N/m}, \qquad\mathbf M=\begin{bmatrix}12.188384 & 0.788953\\0.788953 & 0.610937\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{kg}. \]
\[ \det(\mathbf K-\omega^2\mathbf M)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_{1,\mathrm{local}}=333.7\ \mathrm{Hz},\quad f_{2,\mathrm{local}}=1242.9\ \mathrm{Hz}. \]

The first mode is qualitatively tail-dominated. The second has opposing gear and tail motion, with an interior zero near 317 mm. It is not identified with the report’s second system frequency of 523.67 Hz: the hand model omits other subassemblies, and the report does not supply that system mode’s shape.

Two other checks answer different questions:

  • Original FE mesh sensitivity: four meshes, from 45,845 to 71,836 elements, gave first frequencies of 305–307 Hz. This tests sensitivity to mesh size, not accuracy against the real machine.
  • Internal hand-model verification: a 24-element Euler–Bernoulli discretization of the same assumed beam gave 331.15 and 1227.20 Hz. The two-coordinate estimates were 0.77% and 1.28% higher. This checks the mathematical reduction, not the physical assumptions.

Allowing support translation and tilt

For massless, linear, uncoupled support springs referred to ground, with translational stiffness \(k_y\) and bending-rotation stiffness \(k_\theta\), the tail compliance becomes:

\[ C_{LL,\mathrm{total}}=C_{LL,\mathrm{beam}}+\frac{1}{k_y}+\frac{L^2}{k_\theta}. \]

Two identical radial springs of stiffness \(k\), separated by distance \(d\), give \(k_y=2k\) and \(k_\theta=kd^2/2\) when translation and tilt are measured about the pair’s midpoint. This explains why axial bearing spacing can provide resistance to tilt while the real assembly must still accommodate axial thermal motion. It is an explanatory model, not a reconstruction of the ambiguous Modified 2x case.

No measured bearing stiffness is assigned. Once support motion is included, the interpolation and mass matrix must be rebuilt along with the stiffness matrix; changing stiffness alone would not preserve the same model.

Rev B: rotordynamic sensitivity

The original hand model leaves out rotation. Rev B, added on 13 September 2026, checks one part of that simplification: how cross-sectional rotary inertia and gyroscopic coupling change the frequency of the assumed overhang. It is a new reduced-model calculation, not a new ANSYS solve.

One bending shape in two lateral planes

A spinning cross-section carries angular momentum; tilting its axis couples motion in the two transverse directions. I retain the original tip-normalized static shape \(\phi(s)\), but allow it to move in two perpendicular, stationary planes:

\[ w_x(s,t)=\phi(s)q_x(t),\qquad w_y(s,t)=\phi(s)q_y(t),\qquad \phi(L)=1. \]

This is different from the earlier two-station model. That model retained gear and tail motion in one plane to describe two spatial bending modes. This extension retains one frozen shape in two planes to obtain a forward/backward whirl pair.

The five-section geometry, material properties, ideal clamp, and stiffness stay unchanged. All five equivalent sections are assumed to co-rotate at a prescribed speed. The C-ring remains a translational point mass; its diametral and polar inertias are omitted. There is no added centrifugal-prestress calculation, physical damping, shear correction, or cutting load.

The all-five-rotating assumption differs from the original ANSYS spin assignment, which excluded the bearing stacks and rotary union. The equivalent aluminum tail does not resolve the rotary union’s rotating and stationary internals. This is a sensitivity check, not a reconstruction or verified conservative bound for the real spindle.

Rotary inertia is weighted by slope

Translation is weighted by \(\phi^2\); cross-sectional tilting is weighted by \((\phi')^2\). Using the section area moments \(I_j\) and \(J_{p,j}\):

\[ m_\theta=\sum_j\rho_jI_j\int_{a_j}^{b_j}(\phi')^2\,ds,\qquad g=\sum_j\rho_jJ_{p,j}\int_{a_j}^{b_j}(\phi')^2\,ds. \]

For these circular equivalents, \(J_{p,j}=2I_j\). The original translational generalized mass is \(m_t=0.68843256\ \mathrm{kg}\); the added rotary term is \(m_\theta=0.00971902\ \mathrm{kg}\). Hence:

\[ m=m_t+m_\theta=0.69815157\ \mathrm{kg},\qquad g=2m_\theta=0.01943803\ \mathrm{kg}. \]

These are generalized coefficients for tip displacement, so both have units of kilograms. The gear contributes only about 1.4% of the translational generalized mass but about 34.1% of the gyroscopic coefficient. Its small lateral displacement does not eliminate the effect of its large polar inertia.

Forward and backward whirl

Take right-handed fixed axes \((x,y,s)\), positive shaft spin \(\Omega\) about \(+s\), and \(\mathbf q=[q_x,q_y]^T\). The undamped two-plane equations are:

\[ m\ddot{\mathbf q}+\Omega\mathbf G_1\dot{\mathbf q}+k\mathbf q=\mathbf 0,\qquad \mathbf G_1=\begin{bmatrix}0 & g\\-g & 0\end{bmatrix},\qquad k=k_L. \]

With \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}\) and \(a=g\Omega/(2m)\), the two positive stationary-frame frequencies for \(\Omega\geq0\) follow from the harmonic eigenproblem:

\[ (k-m\omega^2)^2-(g\Omega\omega)^2=0,\qquad \boxed{\omega_\pm=\sqrt{\omega_0^2+a^2}\pm a},\qquad f_\pm=\frac{\omega_\pm}{2\pi}. \]

For this sign convention, the upper branch \(f_+\) is forward whirl, in the same direction as shaft rotation; the lower branch \(f_-\) is backward whirl. At zero spin they coincide. Their separation is a split of one lateral bending family, not an additional spatial mode.

The gyroscopic matrix is skew-symmetric: \(\mathbf G_1^T=-\mathbf G_1\), so \(\dot{\mathbf q}^T\Omega\mathbf G_1\dot{\mathbf q}=0\). It changes the motion without dissipating energy. Gyroscopic coupling is not physical damping.

A nonspinning section can still tilt and contribute to \(m_\theta\). For different section speeds, \(g\Omega\) becomes \(\sum_j g_j\Omega_j\), while all tilting inertia is retained. No numerical spin partition is assigned here because the internal components are unresolved.

What changes at 3000 rpm?

At \(\Omega=314.15927\ \mathrm{rad/s}\), the unchanged stiffness is \(3.08262016\times10^6\ \mathrm{N/m}\) and \(g/m=0.02784214\). The calculation separates the effect of adding rotary inertia from the effect of turning on spin.

Adding rotary inertia lowers the zero-spin result by about 0.70%: sections tilt during bending whether or not the shaft spins. Gyroscopic coupling then shifts the two branches by approximately ±0.208% relative to the extended zero-spin frequency \(f_0=334.43\ \mathrm{Hz}\), not relative to the original 336.78 Hz. The branch separation is about 1.392 Hz.

Within this assumed beam, the small gyroscopic shift supports omitting gyroscopy from the original baseline explanation. It is not a bound on the complete spindle’s rotational effects. The lower branch’s proximity to the separate two-station result of 333.70 Hz is coincidental; they are different reductions.

Campbell diagram: frequency shift versus excitation

A Campbell diagram places frequency branches alongside speed-related excitation lines. For shaft speed \(n\) in rpm, the once-per-revolution line is \(f_{1X}=n/60\). A six-flute tool also introduces \(f_{6X}=6n/60\). At 3000 rpm, these reach 50 Hz and 300 Hz, respectively.

A small gyroscopic shift does not imply weak forcing. The maximum six-flute fundamental is only 7.17 Hz below the reported FE mode. Frequency proximity alone does not establish a large response either: modal forcing, damping, higher harmonics, other modes, and regenerative cutting effects still matter. This diagram does not certify resonance avoidance.

Verification and the remaining FE comparison

Rev B checks the closed-form whirl frequencies against a four-state eigensolution of the same reduced system. It also checks the slope integrals, skew symmetry, coincident branches at zero spin, and branch exchange when spin reverses. These reproduce the calculation; they are not physical validation or a rotating beam-mesh convergence study.

A matched FE study would separate two different discrepancies:

\[ \Delta f_{\mathrm{structure}}=f_{\mathrm{hand}}(0)-f_{\mathrm{FE}}(0),\qquad \Delta f_{\mathrm{speed},\pm}=f_{\mathrm{FE},\pm}(3000)-f_{\mathrm{FE}}(0). \]

Here 0 and 3000 denote shaft speed in rpm. Each comparison needs the corresponding bending family in the same reference frame. No matched zero-speed FE result was supplied, so these differences remain unevaluated.

The next comparison would recover the actual geometry, contacts, bearings, and spinning components; extract modes at zero and 3000 rpm with an intervening sweep; and track complex mode shapes and whirl direction rather than relying on a mode number alone. A gyro-only check must hold mass and stiffness fixed. A total speed-effect study must separately state whether centrifugal prestress and stiffness are recomputed.

Rev B holds stiffness fixed and does not calculate centrifugal stress or its effect on stiffness. The original report’s “damped solver” setting also does not supply a measured damping ratio. Neither omitted effect can be inferred from the ±0.208% gyroscopic result.

What the calculation establishes—and what it does not

The hand model makes the motor-end bending mechanism understandable with a small number of coordinates. Its arithmetic is more tightly checked than its physical inputs: a chosen ±5% change in all axial lengths alone moves the first estimate from about 303 to 370 Hz. That is a sensitivity exercise, not a measured uncertainty interval.

Stronger comparisons would require actual section dimensions and bores, component masses and connections, bearing stiffness and spacing, housing flexibility, and matched zero-speed FE mode shapes. Rev B quantifies rotary inertia and gyroscopic coupling only within the declared simplified model; the complete spindle’s speed dependence, centrifugal prestress, damping, and cutting forces remain unresolved. Neither the original frequency shift nor these hand calculations establish a measured vibration-amplitude reduction, compliance with an operating vibration limit, or stable loaded cutting.
