Spindle Vibration & Deflection Reduction

Role
Structural modeling, FEA, CAD design, solution optimization
Skills
Vibration testing, ANSYS Mechanical, SOLIDWORKS
Timeline
2025 capstone; retrospective calculations: September 2026

Problem Statement

Situation

Can changing where the drill’s shaft is supported reduce shaking and help protect the machine?

Task

Find a better position for the shaft’s supports to reduce shaking during drilling.

Action

I compared shaft-support positions using vibration measurements and computer models.

Result

The model predicted the shaft’s first bending frequency would rise from 307.17 to 383.27 Hz, a 24.8% increase.

Introduction

↑ Back to STAR

Peddinghaus reported excessive spindle vibration while drilling weight-reduction holes in O’Hare Airport I-beams, linking it to long-term damage to the electronics box.

Our capstone used FEA to study how repositioning bearings changed spindle support, motor-end overhang, bending modes, and natural frequencies.

Simplified Model

In September 2026, I revisited the motor-end overhang using a stepped-beam model with varying geometry, materials, and distributed mass.

The gear near the support did not dominate modal inertia; the tail supplied bending flexibility. Frequency estimates resembled the simulation, but assumed geometry limits comparison.

The baseline is an undamped, nonrotating Euler–Bernoulli beam, ideally clamped at the root: \(w(0)=w'(0)=0\). Let \(s\) run from the root to the free tail at \(L\). The stepped sections have bending rigidity \(E(s)I(s)\) and mass per length \(\mu(s)=\rho(s)A(s)\); the C-ring is a point mass \(m_r\) at \(s_r\).

Rayleigh estimate: one bending shape

Use the static tip-load deflection \(v(s)\) as a trial shape, normalized by \(\phi(s)=v(s)/v(L)\). Then \(w(s,t)=\phi(s)q(t)\), where \(q\) is tail displacement. The tip stiffness and shape-weighted mass are:

\[ k_L^{-1}=\int_0^L\frac{(L-s)^2}{E(s)I(s)}\,ds \]
\[ \begin{aligned} m_{\mathrm{eff}}&=\int_0^L\mu(s)\phi(s)^2\,ds\\ &\quad+m_r\phi(s_r)^2 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} m_{\mathrm{eff}}\ddot q+k_Lq=0\\ f_R=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k_L}{m_{\mathrm{eff}}}} \end{gathered} \]

Numerical evaluation. For the assumed nonrotating overhang, the stiffness and shape-weighted mass evaluate to:

\[ \begin{aligned} k_L&\approx 3.08262\times10^6~\mathrm{N/m}\\ m_{\mathrm{eff}}&\approx 0.688433~\mathrm{kg} \end{aligned} \]

Substitute into the frequency expression:

\[ f_R\approx\frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{3.08262\times10^6}{0.688433}}~\mathrm{Hz} \]
\[ \boxed{f_R\approx336.8~\mathrm{Hz}} \]

The \(\phi^2\) weighting counts how much each part moves, rather than treating the whole assembly as a mass at the tip.

Rotating effects: one shape in two planes

Rev B keeps the Rayleigh shape \(\phi\) and stiffness \(k_L\), but allows transverse motion \(w_x=\phi q_x\) and \(w_y=\phi q_y\). This uses one spatial shape in two perpendicular planes. Cross-sectional tilting adds rotary inertia \(m_\theta\) and a gyroscopic coefficient \(g\). Primes denote derivatives with respect to \(s\):

\[ \begin{aligned} m_\theta&=\int_0^L\rho I(\phi')^2\,ds\\ g&=\int_0^L\rho J_p(\phi')^2\,ds\\ m&=m_{\mathrm{eff}}+m_\theta \end{aligned} \]

Here \(I\) and \(J_p\) are cross-sectional area moments; \(J_p=2I\) for the circular equivalents. For constant positive spin \(\Omega\) about \(+s\), using right-handed fixed axes \((x,y,s)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} m\ddot q_x+g\Omega\dot q_y+k_Lq_x&=0\\ m\ddot q_y-g\Omega\dot q_x+k_Lq_y&=0 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{gathered} \omega_0=\sqrt{k_L/m}\\ a=\frac{g\Omega}{2m}\\ \omega_\pm=\sqrt{\omega_0^2+a^2}\pm a\\ f_\pm=\frac{\omega_\pm}{2\pi} \end{gathered} \]

Numerical evaluation. The rotary-inertia integrals give the following values, with the original stiffness unchanged:

\[ \begin{aligned} m_\theta&\approx0.00971902~\mathrm{kg}\\ m&\approx0.69815157~\mathrm{kg}\\ g&\approx0.01943803~\mathrm{kg} \end{aligned} \]

First, include rotary inertia at zero spin:

\[ \begin{aligned} f_0&=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k_L}{m}}\\ &\approx\frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{\frac{3.08262016\times10^6}{0.69815157}}~\mathrm{Hz} \end{aligned} \]
\[ \boxed{f_0\approx334.43~\mathrm{Hz}} \]

Next, set the spin speed to 3000 rpm. Express the gyroscopic term in Hz as \(b=a/(2\pi)\):

\[ \begin{aligned} \Omega&=3000\frac{2\pi}{60}~\mathrm{rad/s}\\ &\approx314.15927~\mathrm{rad/s}\\ b&=\frac{g\Omega}{4\pi m}\\ &\approx0.69605~\mathrm{Hz} \end{aligned} \]

The lower, backward-whirl branch is:

\[ f_-=\sqrt{f_0^2+b^2}-b \]
\[ \boxed{f_-\approx333.73~\mathrm{Hz}} \]

The upper, forward-whirl branch is:

\[ f_+=\sqrt{f_0^2+b^2}+b \]
\[ \boxed{f_+\approx335.13~\mathrm{Hz}} \]

These are stationary-frame predictions, not measurements; boxed results use full-precision calculations.

Here, \(+\)/\(−\) denote forward/backward whirl. Rotary inertia lowers their shared zero-spin frequency relative to Rayleigh. Gyroscopic coupling splits them without damping; the backward branch’s proximity to Rayleigh–Ritz is coincidental.

Initial Testing

↑ Back to STAR

Our on-site campaign combined impact tests, unloaded free-spinning tests, and loaded cutting tests. A tri-axial accelerometer mounted on the spindle housing recorded the response.

Impact tests

Transverse x/y impacts and torsional impacts using a custom torsion bar did not establish quantitative modal conclusions.

Unloaded tests

Twelve 10-second free-spinning trials covered 250–3000 rpm in 250-rpm steps. Wavelet analysis showed a persistent band near 310 Hz in the y-direction, not an experimentally established mode shape.

Loaded tests

Cutting tests used short and long tools at multiple speeds. Long-tool bands varied with speed, with some accelerometer saturation and no single frequency for unloaded-model comparison.

Model references over the unloaded wavelet

Finite Element Analysis

Refined against unloaded tests, ANSYS placed the first mode near the observed band, with bending at the motor-end overhang.

It used grounded outer bearing faces, cylindrical joints, and Coriolis effects at 3000 rpm, with bearing stacks and the rotary union nonrotating. Unloaded-test correlation did not independently validate loaded cutting.

Bearing Modification

We recommended modifying the floating-bearing stack and shifting it 22 mm toward the drive. The preferred configuration raised the first simulated frequency from approximately 307 Hz to 383 Hz, an increase of about 25%. This was a predicted frequency shift, not a measured reduction in operating vibration.

Outcome

↑ Back to STAR

The proposed bearing modification increased the first ANSYS bending-mode frequency from 307.17 to 383.27 Hz (+24.8%), moving it away from the observed ≈310 Hz response band to reduce resonance risk.

The project linked measured vibration, a simulated bending mechanism, and a targeted bearing recommendation. The retrospective calculations added a clearer explanation of why support placement and distributed inertia mattered. Confirming the proposed modification’s operating benefit would require testing the modified spindle under representative cutting conditions.
