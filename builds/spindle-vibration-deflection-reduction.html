Spindle Vibration & Deflection Reduction

Excessive vibration in high-speed milling spindles degrades cut quality, accelerates tool wear, and shortens machine life. In this industry-sponsored capstone project, our team investigated vibration in the vertical spindle of Peddinghaus Corporation’s PeddiSubX-1120 steel mill.

Operational testing and finite-element modeling pointed to bending at the motor-end overhang. We proposed a floating-bearing-stack modification that raised the first simulated modal frequency from 307.17 Hz to 383.27 Hz, approximately 24.8%. The result is a design prediction, not a measured post-installation reduction in vibration.

I later revisited the result with a stepped-beam hand model to understand how geometry, restraint, and distributed mass produce that bending mode. The original project work and the retrospective calculations are presented separately below.

Process

The project began with on-site vibration testing at Peddinghaus’ manufacturing facility. A tri-axial accelerometer recorded housing motion during free-spinning tests from 250 to 3000 rpm and during selected short- and long-tool cutting tests.

Wavelet analysis showed a persistent band near 310 Hz in unloaded operation. Long-tool cutting produced multiple, speed-dependent frequency bands, and portions of those recordings saturated. Impact testing was inconclusive, so the report does not establish a measured full mode shape.

We developed an approximate spindle model in ANSYS Mechanical and refined its contacts and constraints. Its first frequency, 307.17 Hz, was about 0.9% below the unloaded band. The simulated shape concentrated bending at the motor-end tail, guiding comparisons of bracing, material changes, and bearing arrangements.

That agreement is frequency correlation after model refinement, not independent mode-shape validation. The loaded simulation was not successfully validated against the cutting tests.

Original FEA & design recommendation

The preferred concept modified the floating bearing stack and moved it 22 mm toward the drive. This changed the spindle’s support conditions while pursuing the sponsor’s goals of a practical, low-cost solution with limited maintenance.

The report labels the best-performing case “Modified 2x.” Its text is inconsistent about whether this means two bearings total or two additional bearings. I retain the case label rather than infer an exact stack configuration without the original CAD and joint definitions.

For a six-flute tool at 3000 rpm, shaft rotation is 50 Hz and the fundamental tooth-passing frequency is 300 Hz. Moving a mode above that fundamental is useful separation, but higher harmonics and cutting instability remain possible. A frequency shift alone does not establish lower vibration amplitude or chatter-free operation.

Simplified hand calculations: the motor-end overhang

These September 2026 retrospective calculations explain the bending mechanism observed in the spring 2025 project’s simulation. They were not used to choose the original design. Starting with a uniform cantilever gives a first estimate of 255.2 Hz; retaining the stepped geometry and distributed inertia brings the estimate to roughly 334–337 Hz.

The local model begins at the floating bearing and retains a 410 mm overhang, including the gear and tail. I use an ideal lateral clamp, \(w(0)=w'(0)=0\), to represent a rigid limiting support. This constrains bending displacement and slope, not spin about the shaft axis. “Floating” refers to axial freedom; it does not mean that radial support is absent.

From bending flexibility to effective stiffness

Apply a conceptual transverse force \(P\) at the tail. With \(M(s)=P(L-s)\), the strain energy gives the tail compliance. For section endpoints \(a_j,b_j\), measured in meters:

\[ I_j=\frac{\pi D_j^4}{64},\qquad C_{LL}=\frac{\delta_L}{P}=\sum_j\frac{(L-a_j)^3-(L-b_j)^3}{3E_jI_j},\qquad k_L=C_{LL}^{-1}. \]

The five sections give \(C_{LL}=3.24399\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m/N}\) and \(k_L=3.08262\times10^6\ \mathrm{N/m}\). An illustrative 100 N tail force therefore produces 32.44 µm of local tail displacement. This is neither a measured milling load nor a prediction of cutting-tool deflection.

The aluminum stem contributes about 71.5% of this model’s bending compliance; the steel root neck contributes 23.3%, and the large gear just 0.34%. The most visually prominent component is not necessarily the span that controls flexibility.

For a uniform solid beam, \(f_n\propto D L^{-2}\sqrt{E/\rho}\). That first-pass scaling helps explain why overhang geometry and restraint deserve attention before swapping between steel grades with similar elastic properties.

Rayleigh estimate: use the mass that moves

Normalize the static tip-load shape as \(\phi(s)=v(s)/v(L)\). Its squared displacement weights each section’s contribution to kinetic energy:

\[ m_{\mathrm{eff}}=\sum_j\int_{a_j}^{b_j}\rho_j A_j\phi(s)^2\,ds+m_r\phi(s_r)^2, \qquad f_R=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k_L}{m_{\mathrm{eff}}}}. \]

The modeled overhang weighs 10.57 kg, but its tip-normalized generalized mass is only 0.688433 kg. Combined with the effective stiffness, that gives \(f_R=336.8\ \mathrm{Hz}\).

The 6.44 kg gear sits near a nearly stationary root and contributes about 0.0098 kg to this modal mass. The 0.60 kg terminal step moves much more and contributes about 0.44 kg. Putting the entire assembly mass on a spring at the tip would describe a different motion.

“Effective mass” here is the generalized mass for a mode normalized to unit tail motion, not the ANSYS base-excitation participation metric.

Two coordinates, two local modes

A two-coordinate Rayleigh–Ritz reduction retains gear-center displacement at 95 mm and tail displacement at 410 mm. Unit-load beam flexibility supplies the interpolation, rather than assuming two independent lumped masses:

\[ H(s,\xi)=\int_0^{\min(s,\xi)}\frac{(s-u)(\xi-u)}{E(u)I(u)}\,du,\qquad C_{ij}=H(s_i,s_j),\qquad \mathbf K=\mathbf C^{-1}. \]

Integrating the distributed mass with the same interpolation gives a coupled mass matrix. The resulting matrices and free-vibration equation are:

\[ \mathbf K=10^6\begin{bmatrix}593.53793 & -22.53896\\-22.53896 & 3.938513\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{N/m}, \qquad\mathbf M=\begin{bmatrix}12.188384 & 0.788953\\0.788953 & 0.610937\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{kg}. \]
\[ \det(\mathbf K-\omega^2\mathbf M)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_{1,\mathrm{local}}=333.7\ \mathrm{Hz},\quad f_{2,\mathrm{local}}=1242.9\ \mathrm{Hz}. \]

The first mode is qualitatively tail-dominated. The second has opposing gear and tail motion, with an interior zero near 317 mm. It is not identified with the report’s second system frequency of 523.67 Hz: the hand model omits other subassemblies, and the report does not supply that system mode’s shape.

The two-coordinate result is 26.53 Hz higher than the FE reference. It captures a similar bending scale, but geometry, support representation, retained components, and solver conditions differ. The original FE setup included 3000 rpm, Coriolis effects, and a damped modal solver; this local hand model is nonrotating and undamped.

Two other checks answer different questions:

  • Original FE mesh sensitivity: four meshes, from 45,845 to 71,836 elements, gave first frequencies of 305–307 Hz. This tests sensitivity to mesh size, not accuracy against the real machine.
  • Internal hand-model verification: a 24-element Euler–Bernoulli discretization of the same assumed beam gave 331.15 and 1227.20 Hz. The two-coordinate estimates were 0.77% and 1.28% higher. This checks the mathematical reduction, not the physical assumptions.

What does moving the support actually explain?

To isolate one design change, move only the idealized root by 22 mm, shortening the local overhang from 410 to 388 mm while keeping the physical gear and tail geometry unchanged.

The two-coordinate model rises 7.37%, compared with the full FE modification’s 24.77%. The support shift explains the direction of the change, but not its full magnitude. The local root is already perfectly clamped: it has no remaining tilt for an additional bearing to suppress. Bearing spacing, effective restraint, and FE joint scopes can change more than the support’s location alone.

Allowing support translation and tilt

For massless, linear, uncoupled support springs referred to ground, with translational stiffness \(k_y\) and bending-rotation stiffness \(k_\theta\), the tail compliance becomes:

\[ C_{LL,\mathrm{total}}=C_{LL,\mathrm{beam}}+\frac{1}{k_y}+\frac{L^2}{k_\theta}. \]

Two identical radial springs of stiffness \(k\), separated by distance \(d\), give \(k_y=2k\) and \(k_\theta=kd^2/2\) when translation and tilt are measured about the pair’s midpoint. This explains why axial bearing spacing can provide resistance to tilt while the real assembly must still accommodate axial thermal motion. It is an explanatory model, not a reconstruction of the ambiguous Modified 2x case.

No measured bearing stiffness is assigned. Once support motion is included, the interpolation and mass matrix must be rebuilt along with the stiffness matrix; changing stiffness alone would not preserve the same model.

What the calculation establishes—and what it does not

The hand model makes the motor-end bending mechanism understandable with a small number of coordinates. Its arithmetic is more tightly checked than its physical inputs: a chosen ±5% change in all axial lengths alone moves the first estimate from about 303 to 370 Hz. That is a sensitivity exercise, not a measured uncertainty interval.

Stronger comparisons would require actual section dimensions and bores, component masses and connections, bearing stiffness and spacing, housing flexibility, and matched zero-speed FE mode shapes. Rotation, damping, and cutting forces would then need their own treatment. Neither the original frequency shift nor this hand calculation establishes a measured vibration-amplitude reduction, compliance with an operating vibration limit, or stable loaded cutting.
