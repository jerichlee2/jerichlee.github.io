Pigjaw Scaffold–Bone Interface Mechanical Testing

This project explores fixtures for push-out testing at the interface between a biodegradable scaffold and porcine jawbone. Our PLA analysis informed my decision to build the metal push-out fixture shown here. The work connects fixture mechanics and material selection with the practical design of the test assembly.

Revision C compares the hand calculations with the supplied PLA results from ANSYS Mechanical at the same assumed 250 N load. The 60 mm bridge predicts 31.77 µm of movement, while the final FE model gives 131.37 µm of maximum downward annular motion—about 2.63 times the chosen 50 µm target. This revision adds model comparisons and interpretation; it retains the original calculations and introduces no new simulation.

The numerical results identify a compliance concern under the stated assumptions. They do not establish observed breakage, a safe operating load, or experimental validation of the fixture.

Geometry and loading

The supplied solid has a 120 × 140 × 30 mm envelope, but only a 15 mm central web above the underside relief. A 25 mm opening is surrounded by a slotted annulus with two narrow connections, each represented by a minimum ligament width of about 6.08 mm. Using the overall 30 mm height as the bending thickness would overestimate the rectangular-section bending stiffness by a factor of eight.

The 250 N force is a literature-context benchmark, not a measured porcine test load. The 50 µm motion budget and PLA screening properties are analysis assumptions. Rev C retains 500 N and 1000 N as sensitivity cases.

A separate fit issue remains: a nominal 25 mm implant in a 25 mm opening has zero radial clearance. A provisional 0.7 mm radial-clearance starting point would imply a 26.4 mm opening before tolerances, but it is not a validated requirement for this porcine construct. Any enlargement also reduces the annulus width and needs a new structural check.

Simplified hand calculations

The starting point is an equivalent bridge: replace the deck with a simply supported beam carrying a central load of 250 N. Assume the bottom lands are backed by a rigid base, with effective supports 60 mm apart and a 15 mm web thickness. Removing the full 44.3 mm slot envelope from the 120 mm width gives a reduced beam width of 75.7 mm.

1. Section properties and loading

For the reduced rectangular section, the cross-sectional area and second moment of area are:

\[ \begin{aligned} A_g &= b_g h = 1135.5\ \mathrm{mm^2} \\ I_g &= \frac{b_g h^3}{12} = 21290.6\ \mathrm{mm^4} \end{aligned} \]

The centered load splits equally between the supports. The maximum bending moment occurs at midspan:

\[ \begin{aligned} R_A = R_B &= \frac{F}{2} = 125\ \mathrm{N} \\ M_{\max} &= \frac{FL}{4} = 3750\ \mathrm{N\,mm} \end{aligned} \]

The corresponding nominal global bending stress is:

\[ \sigma_{g,\max} = \frac{M_{\max}(h/2)}{I_g} = 1.321\ \mathrm{MPa} \]

2. Bending plus shear deflection

Using the assumed PLA properties \(E = 2000\ \mathrm{MPa}\) and \(\nu = 0.35\), the shear modulus is \(G = E/[2(1+\nu)] = 740.7\ \mathrm{MPa}\). The rectangular-section shear correction is \(\kappa = 5/6\). Midspan movement includes both bending and transverse shear:

\[ \begin{aligned} \delta_b &= \frac{FL^3}{48EI_g} = 0.02642\ \mathrm{mm} \\ \delta_s &= \frac{FL}{4\kappa GA_g} = 0.00535\ \mathrm{mm} \end{aligned} \]
\[ \boxed{\delta_g = \delta_b + \delta_s = 31.77\ \mathrm{\mu m}} \]

Shear contributes about 17% of the total, so it is worth retaining for this short bridge with \(L/h = 4\). The estimate is below the chosen 50 µm motion budget, but it describes only the equivalent bridge—not all the motion in the slotted fixture.

3. What the bridge leaves out

Separate local checks assume the load enters the annulus and divides equally between its two 6.08 mm ligaments. At 250 N, the average contact pressure is 0.490 MPa and the average ligament shear stress is 1.371 MPa. Applying a rectangular-section peak factor of 1.5 and an assumed concentration multiplier of 2 gives a shear screening value of 4.113 MPa.

Local bending is more sensitive to the unknown load path: effective moment arms of 5, 10, and 15 mm give screened stresses of 5.48, 10.97, and 16.45 MPa, respectively, with an assumed concentration multiplier of 2. These are sensitivity cases, not measured moment arms.

The bridge does not resolve annulus bending, ligament deformation, or actual contact load sharing. Its reduced width does not guarantee an upper bound on real fixture motion, and passing this calculation alone does not qualify the fixture. The full PLA model below predicts 131.37 µm of maximum downward annular motion at the same 250 N load.

See pages 5–6 of Rev C for the complete global and local hand calculations.

PLA fixture model

The analysis uses ANSYS Mechanical 2025 R2 and a single isotropic, linear-elastic PLA fixture body with E = 2000 MPa and ν = 0.35. A downward pressure of 0.4901 MPa on the 510.1083 mm² top annulus supplies approximately 250 N total. Bone, scaffold, and interface failure are not modeled.

The two lowest bottom lands have compression-only plate support. Four 16 mm outer-diameter washer patches restrain vertical motion while allowing in-plane movement; two additional locators remove in-plane rigid motion. These idealized hold-downs include no bolt preload, washer bending, or bolt stretch. Contact uses a manual normal-stiffness factor of 10, and large deflection is off.

Rev C retains the recorded setup and supplied result screenshots; it is not an independent solver rerun. The final solver project and complete logs were not included in that record.

Hand calculations versus FEA

The bridge replaces the slotted solid with a constant-section beam, a central resultant, and simple supports. The FE model resolves the connected ring and ligaments, distributes pressure over the annulus, and includes compression-only backing and idealized washer hold-downs. Beam midspan movement and maximum annular nodal movement are different response measures.

Rev C makes the comparison convention explicit. For each stated quantity, the final FE value is the numerical reference:

\[ e_{\mathrm{HC|FE}} = \frac{q_{\mathrm{HC}}-q_{\mathrm{FE}}}{|q_{\mathrm{FE}}|}\,100\% \]

A negative percentage means the hand value is lower. Using FEA as the denominator does not make it experimental truth.

The compression-only and bending-only rows are deliberately incomplete screens, not complete fixture predictions. Nor should the separate global and local estimates simply be added together. All four rows use 250 N; the separate 127.08 µm bridge result at 1000 N is not evidence of same-load agreement.

The 100 mm span was a sensitivity, not a fitted result

The original hand report already included the longer-span case. Its 131.23 µm prediction is only 0.14 µm below the FE maximum, but mounting-row spacing does not establish the bearing span. Agreement in one number does not validate the assumed support span, load path, or deformation mechanism.

These percentages describe model discrepancies, not experimentally measured prediction errors. Both models share unverified material and loading assumptions. Rev C, pages 14–15.

Local stress: useful screening, not parameter validation

The global bridge's 1.321 MPa nominal bending stress is numerically 76.31% below the 5.5769 MPa averaged FE principal peak. Those are not matching stress definitions. The more targeted ligament screen assumes two equal load paths and an effective moment arm \(\ell\):

\[ M_\ell = \frac{F\ell}{2}, \qquad \sigma_{\ell,\mathrm{screen}} = K_t\frac{3F\ell}{b_\ell h^2}, \qquad K_t=2 \]

These are screening comparisons between locally scaled bending stresses and three-dimensional principal peaks, not matched-component stress errors. Averaged and unaveraged peaks are two displays of one FE solution, not bounds on the true stress. Rev C, page 16.

Mesh, stress, and equilibrium checks

The final M2 mesh contains 941,817 quadratic tetrahedral elements. Its 0.5 mm sizing is local to the four rounded slot-end walls, not the entire fixture. M0, M1, and M2 share the idealized bolted restraint and factor-10 contact setting. Rev C reports both refinement steps, with each change referenced to the preceding mesh:

The M1-to-M2 annular maximum changes from 0.13134 to 0.13137 mm, only 0.03 µm. That is sensitivity to the final local refinement, not proof of physical accuracy to 0.023%.

Stress convergence is less complete. The final maximum principal tensile peaks are 5.5769 MPa averaged and 6.0484 MPa unaveraged. Their M1-to-M2 changes are +3.21% and −5.72%, respectively; the latter misses the chosen 5% successive-change criterion. Both final values are below the assumed 10 MPa normal allowance, but print anisotropy, interlayer weakness, creep, and cracking are absent, and the low-quality-element location relative to the stress peak remains unresolved. This is not a complete strength pass.

The gap between unaveraged and averaged peak values narrows from 18.72% at M1 to 8.45% at M2, using the averaged peak as denominator. The maxima can occur at different locations, so this is not a local error estimate or an uncertainty interval.

The signed vertical reactions provide another consistency check: +355.55 N at the plate and −105.50 N across all four washer patches give +250.05 N against the nominal −250 N load. The 0.05 N residual is 0.020% of the load, using rounded readouts. It is a vertical balance check, not a full moment-balance or solver audit.

Model discrepancy, numerical sensitivity, and experimental accuracy answer different questions. Neither the small force residual nor the final mesh change establishes displacement accuracy in a physical test. Rev C, pages 12–13.

What the model differences do—and do not—explain

The broad bridge assumes the central resultant has already reached its equivalent section. It omits the local deformation required to transfer load through the annulus and narrow connections. That is a plausible contributor to the discrepancy, not an isolated or quantified explanation. Geometry, loading, support conditions, and the location of the reported response all differ.

The FE annular extrema span 76.435 µm. This establishes nonuniform vertical motion, but a rigid surface undergoing rotation can also have different Y-displacements across it. The extrema alone cannot separate ring warping from translation or rotation, establish specimen tilt, or assign the entire 99.60 µm hand–FE difference to ligament flexibility.

Support and contact checks were limited comparisons

An earlier unclamped result was recorded as 0.13719 mm, versus 0.13024 mm for the bolted, program-controlled case—a 5.07% decrease. The earlier number survives as a transcription and mesh equivalence was not established, so the difference cannot be attributed solely to hold-downs.

On the coarse bolted mesh, changing the contact setting from program-controlled to manual factor 10 reduced maximum annular motion from 0.13024 to 0.12942 mm, a 0.630% decrease. This is one contact-parameter sensitivity check, not evidence that every support or contact effect is negligible.

The hand calculations identified useful features and sensitivities—the 15 mm web, approximately 6.08 mm connections, shear contribution, local bending, and support span. Passing the global bridge screen still did not establish that the full slotted fixture met the selected motion target. Rev C, page 17.

What the revision establishes

The unchanged PLA concept does not meet the selected 250 N / 50 µm screen. That identifies a modeled compliance concern; it does not demonstrate fracture or prove that machined metal is mandatory. The same-geometry hand comparisons give aluminum about 34 times PLA’s flexural stiffness and steel about 100 times, but no aluminum or steel FEA or physical qualification is included.

Before experimental use, the next steps are to establish the actual force and accuracy requirements, verify specimen clearance and contact, characterize the print and mounting conditions, resolve local stress uncertainty, and measure the complete assembly’s motion under a representative dummy-specimen load within separately established limits.
