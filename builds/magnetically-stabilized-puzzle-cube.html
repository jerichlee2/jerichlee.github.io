Introduction

As a competitive speedcuber, I wanted the control of a magnetic cube without making it harder to turn. Magnetic attraction gives each turn a distinct click and helps the layers line up. This project grew from a simple question about the cube’s internal layout: could moving the magnets preserve that feel while improving control?

Can moving the magnets make a puzzle cube easier to turn and help its sides line up?

Process

I wanted to preserve the magnetic click while making turns easier to control. Moving magnet mass toward the axis lowers rotational inertia, but it also changes the magnetic pull and its leverage. My original idea was to place the magnets closer to the core and help the layer finish each turn.

I built a Prusa MK3 from a kit, fueled by the included gummy bears, and began printing cubes in my garage. After trying different placements, I put the magnets in the roots of the edges and corners. The later analysis below tests that idea and shows why lower inertia does not automatically mean less overshoot.

Nonlinear Analysis

Think of alignment as the bottom of a bowl: the root-mounted model has a wider, gentler bowl, while the side-mounted model has a narrower, firmer one. The first can guide the layer home from farther away; the second limits its first swing past alignment.

These are model predictions, not prototype measurements. The study treats one turning layer as rigid and compares assumed magnet layouts, not a measured commercial cube; individual-piece wobble, contact, and dry friction are not included.

Reduced-order system model

I modeled the positive-z layer—four complete edges, four corners, and one center—as a rigid body about a fixed vertical axis, with angle measured from alignment and the remainder fixed. Inertia includes pieces, magnets, and adhesive mass; magnets follow piece ownership. This uncalibrated 1-DOF model omits dry friction, contact/impact, axial motion, compliance, and independent cubie motion.

The state and torque balance are:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf{x}&=(\theta,\omega),\\ \dot{\theta}&=\omega,\\ J\dot{\omega}&=\tau(t)-c\omega-U'(\theta). \end{aligned} \]

Free releases are unforced. Prescribed harmonic excitation uses 30 Hz for periodic-orbit analysis and 10–80 Hz for the frequency sweep:

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau_{\mathrm{free}}&=0,\\ \tau_{\mathrm{forced}}(t)&=0.002\cos(2\pi ft)\ \mathrm{N\,m}. \end{aligned} \]

The finite-cylinder surface-charge model uses prescribed uniform magnetization and sums 560 moving–fixed pair energies; same-assembly interactions are constant. Position and orientation changes produce the nonlinear torque:

\[ \begin{aligned} M&=\frac{B_r}{\mu_0},\\ \tau_{\mathrm{mag}}(\theta)&=-U'(\theta). \end{aligned} \]

Reflection and quarter-turn symmetry extend the 0–45° grid, sampled at 0.1°, into a periodic C² cubic spline. All simulations use this full magnetic potential. Alignment curvature defines the local linearization; a separate even-potential fit over 0–1° gives diagnostic coefficients, with angles in radians:

\[ \begin{aligned} k_0&=U''(0),\\ U'(\theta)&\approx k_{\mathrm{fit}}\theta+\alpha\theta^3+\beta\theta^5. \end{aligned} \]

Both layouts have positive alignment curvature. For unforced motion:

\[ \begin{aligned} U'(\theta_*)&=0,\\ J\lambda^2+c\lambda+U''(\theta_*)&=0,\\ V&=\tfrac12J\omega^2+U(\theta)-U(\theta_*),\\ \dot V&=-c\omega^2. \end{aligned} \]

The model establishes local asymptotic stability, not global alignment capture. LaSalle’s argument applies within a compact invariant sublevel set containing no other equilibrium; both layouts also admit an attracting 45° equilibrium. The fitted coefficients characterize local behavior but do not replace the full potential in simulation.

Capture, Overshoot, and Decay

Local stiffness and inertia explain small-amplitude frequency differences; damped spirals and small-amplitude overshoot also occur in a linear model. Finite barriers and competing capture destinations require the full nonlinear potential.

Simulation results

01 / 03

Capture: the same 15° release finds two different resting positions

Released from rest at 15°, the root layout starts inside its 20.47° saddle and returns to 0°; the side layout starts beyond its 11.84° saddle and settles at 45°. Extrapolating the local quadratic potential about 0° would predict alignment capture for both, missing the competing 45° attractor.

Overshoot: a softer pull lets the first swing travel farther

Decay: each pass through alignment loses speed

Near alignment, the next crossing retains about 75.1% of the speed for root and 86.1% for side. Root loses more speed per cycle, but its cycles take longer; this is not the same advantage per second.

Outcome

The design received U.S. Patent No. 10,583,351 B2 in 2020. The later nonlinear model gives a testable tradeoff: root placement recovers from a 15° stationary release that the side layout does not, while allowing greater first overshoot in the matched release tests.

Measuring torque against angle and recording real release motion would test whether those predictions hold for the prototype; this rigid-layer study does not establish individual-piece stability.
