Problem Statement

Situation

Can moving the magnets make a puzzle cube easier to turn and help its sides line up?

Task

Keep the cube’s magnetic click while making fast turns easier to control and helping its sides line up.

Action

I printed prototypes with magnets near the center, then compared their modeled motion with a side-mounted layout.

Result

In the model, a 15° release from rest returned to alignment with root-mounted magnets but settled at 45° with side-mounted magnets.

Introduction

↑ Back to STAR

As a competitive speedcuber, I wanted the control of a magnetic cube without making it harder to turn. Magnetic attraction gives each turn a distinct click and helps the layers line up. This project grew from a simple question about the cube’s internal layout: could moving the magnets preserve that feel while improving control?

Process

↑ Back to STAR

I wanted to preserve the magnetic click while making turns easier to control. Moving magnet mass toward the axis lowers rotational inertia, but it also changes the magnetic pull and its leverage. My original idea was to place the magnets closer to the core and help the layer finish each turn.

I built a Prusa MK3 from a kit, fueled by the included gummy bears, and began printing cubes in my garage. After trying different placements, I put the magnets in the roots of the edges and corners. The later analysis below tests that idea and shows why lower inertia does not automatically mean less overshoot.

Nonlinear Analysis

↑ Back to STAR

Think of alignment as the bottom of a bowl: the root-mounted model has a wider, gentler bowl, while the side-mounted model has a narrower, firmer one. The first can guide the layer home from farther away; the second limits its first swing past alignment.

These are model predictions, not prototype measurements. The study treats one turning layer as rigid and compares assumed magnet layouts, not a measured commercial cube; individual-piece wobble, contact, and dry friction are not included.

The model behind the motion

Jθ̈ + cθ̇ + U′(θ) = τ(t)

The magnetic potential includes 48 finite cylindrical magnets: 20 move and 28 stay fixed, giving 560 changing interactions. Both layouts use the same assumed damping, c = 2 × 10−4 N·m·s/rad; the release cases have no applied torque.

Root placement lowers layer inertia by 6.95%, but lowers local magnetic stiffness by about 71%. That softer restoring action explains its larger first overshoot. Near either aligned minimum, energy decreases as dV/dt = −cω², establishing local return in this reduced model, not return from every starting condition.

Capture, Overshoot, and Decay

Where a layer ends up, how far it first swings, and how quickly its motion fades are three different questions.

Capture: the same 15° release finds two different resting positions

Overshoot: a softer pull lets the first swing travel farther

Decay: each pass through alignment loses speed

Near alignment, the next crossing retains about 75.1% of the speed for root and 86.1% for side. Root loses more speed per cycle, but its cycles take longer; this is not the same advantage per second.

Response to Repeated Forcing

A separate test applies a repeating 2 mN·m torque. At 30 Hz, the root response is much larger; at 55 Hz, the side response is larger. Neither layout has the smaller response at every frequency.

Outcome

↑ Back to STAR

The design received U.S. Patent No. 10,583,351 B2 in 2020. The later nonlinear model gives a testable tradeoff: root placement recovers from a 15° stationary release that the side layout does not, while allowing greater first overshoot in the matched release tests.

Measuring torque against angle and recording real release motion would test whether those predictions hold for the prototype; this rigid-layer study does not establish individual-piece stability.

This cube design was used to help break the world record for the fastest single 3×3×3 Rubik’s Cube solve: 2.76 seconds (Teodor Zajder, GLS Big Cubes Gdańsk 2026).
